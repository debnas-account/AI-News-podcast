# Script

Good morning, it's Sunday, November 22. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. UN talks on so called killer robots end without a mandate for a binding treaty. The Lean community certifies the computer proof of the Riemann hypothesis. And two large deals show how AI is reshaping chips and cybersecurity.

[pauze]

We start in Geneva. A week of talks under the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons ended early on Saturday without agreement to negotiate a legally binding treaty on lethal autonomous weapons. The Guardian reports that the talks collapsed just before five in the morning, after all night negotiations, and that diplomats accused Russia of filibustering. Because the convention's 128 parties decide by consensus, one objection is enough to block a decision.

What was adopted instead is much weaker. According to blue News, citing the news agencies AFP and dpa, states agreed to draft an international instrument on lethal autonomous weapons systems within two years. Some countries objected to the word negotiate and would only accept draft. Politico Europe puts it as the effort being delayed to 2028, and notes that Russia was not the only opponent. The Guardian says the United States was reluctant to commit to a binding protocol, and that the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, South Korea and Turkey also preferred a nonbinding agreement. More than 100 states wanted a new law.

The criticism is sharp. Stop Killer Robots says the delay puts civilians at risk. Brazil's ambassador remarked that some states are hiding behind Russia. And as Brazil's delegation pointed out, these are not weapons of the future but systems already in use. The question now is whether the countries pushing for a treaty will move outside the convention, as happened with the ban on cluster munitions in 2008.

[pauze]

Next, an update on the Riemann hypothesis. On Friday, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he had proved it with help from Claude, in the form of a Lean library of some 17 million lines of code. The new development is that the Lean and Mathlib communities have now published a joint statement, endorsed by the Lean F R O and the Mathlib Initiative. They write that they certify that both the statement and the proof are correct. Concretely, the code proves Mathlib's own carefully vetted definition of the hypothesis, and several independent proof checkers accepted it.

Andrew Sutherland of MIT keeps a public verification record. It shows that all of the more than 175 thousand modules compile without errors, that the main theorems rely only on Lean's three standard axioms, and that an independent sandboxed build reproduced the result. Some checks were still running overnight.

The caveats matter. This certifies the formal artifact, not a human understanding of the argument. The Lean community says it looks forward to mathematicians explaining the new ideas, and according to The Decoder, the argument would run to more than 250 thousand pages if printed. The generalized version covered here is limited to so called Dirichlet L functions. Meanwhile, The Decoder reports that the competing all human draft posted by Stanford's Ravi Vakil on Friday drew reports of problems within hours, some of them found with AI models. If the Claude proof holds, mathematics may face a new situation: a result that is machine verified, but that no human yet fully understands.

[pauze]

Now, chips. Amazon is closing in on a deal to buy the AI startup Decart for up to 7.5 billion dollars, the Financial Times reports, adding that the acquisition would help Amazon Web Services compete with Nvidia in the race for computing power. The Israeli business site Calcalist first reported the terms on Wednesday: more than 7 billion dollars in cash, stock and deferred payments, with additional payments tied to targets bringing the total to nearly 7.5 billion. Neither company has confirmed a deal.

Decart builds software that makes it easier to move AI workloads between different chips, and it develops world models that generate interactive environments and real time video. According to Calcalist, its revenue is estimated in the low tens of millions of dollars, and it was valued at 4 billion dollars in May. Anthropic was in talks to buy it earlier, but withdrew in September.

The logic, as the Financial Times frames it, is competition with Nvidia. Software that makes it easier to switch between chips could lower the barrier for customers to move to alternatives, such as the chips Amazon designs itself. That would explain a high price for a company with modest revenue.

[pauze]

Then, cybersecurity. CrowdStrike is in talks to buy Zscaler, according to Bloomberg and Reuters, both citing people familiar with the matter. The companies have not confirmed the talks, and they could still end without a deal. According to the trade publication BankInfoSecurity, Zscaler was valued at about 32 billion dollars, and a deal could cost upwards of 40 billion. Zscaler shares rose about 16 percent after hours, while CrowdStrike fell almost 5 percent. CrowdStrike's largest acquisition to date cost less than 630 million dollars.

Bloomberg frames this as part of a wave of multibillion dollar takeovers, as the biggest security firms race to defend against increasingly sophisticated attacks powered by AI. BankInfoSecurity points to rival Palo Alto Networks, which has proposed a 4.5 billion dollar purchase of Applied Compute. The pattern is that security is consolidating into a few large platforms. That may make defense easier to manage for customers, but it raises the question of how much risk ends up concentrated with a handful of vendors.

[pauze]

Next, the fallout around Nvidia and the markets. Bloomberg reports that California's investigation into whether Nvidia illegally sold advanced chips to China and misled investors also covers potential criminal violations of state law. That raises the stakes beyond the securities questions raised when the attorney general issued a subpoena on Thursday. No charges have been reported.

The market turmoil is also spreading to Asia. According to Bloomberg, Taiwan widened its curbs on short selling and said it has been buying shares itself, after its benchmark index had its worst weekly fall in 35 years. When a government intervenes like this, it signals that doubts about the AI boom are no longer just a Wall Street story.

[pauze]

In brief.

Etsy is going back to the drawing board on a new policy that would have let it use sellers' content to develop and train AI, Bright reports. Sellers reacted angrily, and some reportedly closed their shops.

China's National Data Administration plans to improve standards for measuring and billing AI tokens, and for quality assessment, according to Bloomberg. It is a sign that Beijing sees tokens as a basic unit of the computing economy.

And researchers at Nvidia argue that very large context windows are often unnecessary, The Decoder reports. In their tests, periodic summaries and targeted keyword search usually matched the results of processing the full context, at a fraction of the cost.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. There is no major Dutch AI news this weekend.

[pauze]

That's it for this Sunday. Thanks for listening, and see you tomorrow.
