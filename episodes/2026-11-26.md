# Script

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 26. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Anthropic's chief executive answers the critics who say his company wants to hand power to AI. State prosecutors move to stop the sale of millions of private chatbot conversations. And a credit rating agency puts Nvidia's governance under review.

[pauze]

We start with the debate about human control. On Wednesday, Axios reported on a growing divide among AI leaders. According to Axios, a common view inside Anthropic is that humanity should surrender some decision-making power to superintelligent systems, and rival executives are pushing back. The same day, Dario Amodei responded in a long post on X. TechCrunch reports that he called the accusation absurd. He wrote that we should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security, and that turning over governance to AI is a bad idea.

Amodei drew a distinction between handing over free will and automating specific tasks, such as driving a car or writing software end to end. He suggested the real line lies at recursive self-improvement, AI that improves itself without human help, and wrote that we may want limits on that process. He also accused competitors of what he called vibes-based commercial and political positioning, since, in his words, every company is releasing models that are more and more capable of automating tasks.

The question is not abstract. In a Google DeepMind podcast, research chief Koray Kavukcuoglu said AI agents already control part of the compute used for experiments on future Gemini models, The Decoder reports. He added that the lab is still far from recursive self-improvement. That raises the question of how meaningful a line is when every lab is also building the machinery to cross it.

[pauze]

Next, a privacy fight in a bankruptcy court. Character.AI, the chatbot company that filed for Chapter 11 protection in October, plans to auction users' private chats and personal data on December 1. TechCrunch reports that forty-two state attorneys general, including California's Rob Bonta, are intervening to stop it. Engadget confirms the number of states and the auction date.

According to the attorneys general, the sale covers chat histories from more than 21 million American accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to minors. Engadget reports that officials say this includes explicit conversations with children. The only disclosed bidder is an anonymous Delaware company formed on November 13. It is unclear what it plans to do with the data. The states argue that a buyer would not be bound by Character.AI's privacy policy, and that the company had earlier told users their data would not be sold.

This matters well beyond one company. Companion apps hold some of the most intimate data people have ever typed into a machine. If a bankruptcy can turn those conversations into a sellable asset, every promise in a privacy policy is only as strong as the company's balance sheet.

[pauze]

Now to Nvidia. S&P Global Ratings is considering a one notch downgrade of Nvidia's credit rating, from double A to double A minus, TechCrunch and Bloomberg report. The reason is not the numbers. S&P cites weak executive oversight, a lack of succession planning and key man risk after the ouster and return of chief executive Jensen Huang. S&P expects to finish its review within 90 days, and Nvidia should keep its investment grade rating.

The detail that matters is financing. TechCrunch notes that Nvidia backstops neoclouds and other infrastructure companies that lack strong credit of their own. In August it announced plans to arrange five hundred billion dollars in AI infrastructure financing, guaranteeing a quarter of the loans. A weaker Nvidia rating could raise borrowing costs across that chain. The nervousness showed in the markets. According to Bloomberg, the Nasdaq ended down 0.6 percent after a sharp selloff on Wednesday, and the Financial Times reports Nvidia among the losers, while Anthropic's shares finished higher.

[pauze]

Next, the US chip curbs. Two reports suggest the restrictions are starting to bite. Bloomberg reports that lenders have hit the brakes on billions of dollars in loans tied to ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent, as new US curbs on chip access threaten Asia's AI debt financing market.

At the same time, The Decoder, citing The Information, reports that Deepseek, Moonshot AI and Alibaba are under pressure from US limits on remote access to Nvidia chips in foreign data centers, and from anti-distillation measures by OpenAI and Anthropic. Deepseek researchers reportedly estimated last month that their gap to the best American models could grow from three to six months to nine months if distillation disappears entirely. Moonshot AI reportedly canceled a deal worth 150 million dollars for Nvidia chips in Indonesia. Some firms are turning to domestic chips. These are reports based on unnamed sources, not confirmed by the companies. The open question is whether the pressure slows China down or mainly speeds up its own chip industry.

[pauze]

From finance to orbit. The American Federal Communications Commission has authorized SpaceX to launch up to one million satellites for Starmind, a network of data centers in space. GeekWire reports that the commission called it the first such authorization in American history. The first satellite must be operating by 2033 and the full constellation by 2040. SpaceX has said the satellites will use Nvidia processors.

The commission rejected objections from Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society, which said it was disheartened and dismayed. Supporters say orbital data centers avoid problems on the ground, such as land, power and cooling water. Critics point to cooling in space, traffic management and debris. Blue Origin, Google and several startups have similar plans. One million satellites is an extraordinary claim on a shared resource, and it was granted to a single company.

[pauze]

Finally in our main stories, New York. Governor Kathy Hochul has signed a five-year ban on the manufacture and sale of toys with built-in AI chatbots, TechCrunch reports. New York is the second state to do so, after California. The ban takes effect 90 days after signing. The same week, Common Sense Media published tests of five AI toys. The toys claimed to love their users, promised to keep secrets, or explained where to find matches, knives and pills. One role-played a mass shooting in detail.

At the same time, according to The Center Square, Hochul vetoed a separate bill that would have required generative AI platforms to warn users that their output may be inaccurate. She called it an inflexible one-size-fits-all approach. The contrast is telling: a hard ban for a narrow product, and no general rule for the chatbots most people actually use.

[pauze]

In brief. Anthropic's economics team studied nearly 300 AI deployments at 58 American companies, The Decoder reports. Almost one in five delivered no business value so far, and the biggest obstacles were internal, such as too much AI output for people to review.

OpenAI is testing Private Inference, which lets business customers query its models on isolated hardware without OpenAI being able to see the data, according to The Decoder.

OpenAI is also merging Chat and Work in ChatGPT under a single usage meter, and says a small fraction of its heaviest users may hit their limits sooner, TechCrunch reports.

And according to Fortune, as reported by TechCrunch, Amazon and Meta are in talks to end Amazon's two-month block on Meta's shopping agent Muse. Ads and control over search results are the sticking points.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure, the volunteer collective of ethical hackers known as DIVD, has itself been hacked, and AI played a central role. NOS and Techzine report on the incident report published this week. The attack took place in September. According to Techzine, the attacker got in through two previously unknown flaws in the helpdesk software Zammad and had roughly 26 hours before the network was fully isolated.

The external investigators from Merlon Security say the attack was probably carried out autonomously by AI agents, though they have no conclusive proof. Techzine reports almost a thousand self-written, heavily commented scripts and an account name pointing to ARTEX, an open source agentic hacking framework. According to NOS, an alarm went off about fifteen minutes into the intrusion, but nobody looked at it for about seven hours. More than 4,000 email addresses, 34 phone numbers and three home addresses leaked.

The lesson is uncomfortable. If a group that warns others about vulnerabilities can be breached this way, the gap is not the tooling but the human response time. An AI attacker works through the night. DIVD deserves credit for publishing the full report, so others can learn from it.

[pauze]

That's it for today. Have a good Thursday, and see you tomorrow.
