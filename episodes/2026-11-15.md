# Script

Good morning, it's Sunday, November 15. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. A quieter Sunday, so a slightly shorter episode. Today: the fallout at Nvidia after Jensen Huang's exit, the funding hole it leaves for AI startups, Dario Amodei on the compute bet behind Anthropic's IPO, and OpenAI reshuffling its robotics work.

[pauze]

We start with Nvidia. Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive on Thursday, four days after admitting to an affair with a chip executive whom United States intelligence suspects of being a Chinese spy, TechCrunch reports. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is now interim chief executive.

Now there is pushback. A petition asking the board to reinstate Huang had more than 115,000 signatures by Saturday afternoon, according to TechCrunch. The petition site lists almost six thousand current Nvidia employees, more than a thousand former employees and more than sixty-three thousand shareholders. Elon Musk, Replit chief executive Amjad Masad, Epic Games chief executive Tim Sweeney and Y Combinator chief executive Garry Tan are among the supporters. The signers say Huang was removed without meaningful due process and want him back while an independent investigation establishes the facts.

Meanwhile the search for a successor has begun. According to the Financial Times, as reported by Bright, Arm chief executive Rene Haas has been approached. These are said to be exploratory talks, not a formal offer, and other candidates have also been approached. Nvidia told the Financial Times it is still selecting an executive search firm and has not yet officially spoken with candidates.

TechCrunch also asks where Nvidia's security team was. Its longtime chief security officer stepped down in August for health reasons. TechCrunch stresses that nothing public links that change to the case, and Nvidia did not answer its questions. A retired intelligence officer quoted in the piece hopes the affair will be a wake-up call for American industry. The broader point stands: at a company that makes the strategic chips of this era, the personal security of the leadership is no longer a side issue.

[pauze]

Huang's exit is not only a governance story. It is also a funding story. TechCrunch spoke to investors and founders who describe a big hole in startup financing. According to PitchBook data cited by TechCrunch, Nvidia took part in 105 funding rounds this year alone, often as the largest investor. Several investors say many of those decisions came from Huang himself.

Nvidia's bets went mostly to neoclouds, inference providers and developers of open weight models. It backed CoreWeave before its listing, joined Crusoe's 1.4 billion dollar Series E, and holds stakes in Mistral AI, Lambda and Black Forest Labs. One investor calls Kress much more conservative and doubts she will commit the same amounts. A venture capitalist from Dawn Capital warns that the gap could be especially hard to fill in Europe.

There is a flip side, and TechCrunch names it too. By writing such generous checks, Nvidia effectively tied startups to its own chips, reinforcing its dominance. A more cautious Nvidia could leave room for Google, Amazon, Microsoft and AMD. Few investors expect the slowdown to halt the broader AI boom on its own.

Governments in Asia are watching as well, TechCrunch reports. South Korea's science ministry says Nvidia has reaffirmed its plan to supply 260,000 advanced chips to the country. Japan says it will monitor the company closely. And according to Reuters, cited by TechCrunch, more than three billion dollars in Chinese computing projects that rely on restricted Nvidia chips now face uncertainty.

[pauze]

Next, Anthropic, which plans to list on November 19. Speaking at a JPMorgan investor event in New York on Friday, chief executive Dario Amodei said there is a huge uncertainty in what is going to happen next year, The Decoder reports. He called compute the deepest business dilemma the company faces. Capacity has to be bought one to two years ahead, before anyone knows whether revenue will justify it. Buy too little and you lose customers. Buy too much and you can financially overextend yourself.

The numbers are large. According to Bloomberg, as reported by The Decoder, Anthropic has committed nearly 901 billion dollars, mostly to compute and chips, with roughly 71 billion coming due by September 2027. The company aims to raise up to 64.4 billion dollars through the listing and another 26 billion through a private placement. The offering is reportedly about six times oversubscribed. Company filings put the annualized revenue run rate at 81 billion dollars at the end of October.

Amodei said the main reason to go public is a cash buffer that widens the margin for error. It is unusual for a chief executive to name the core weakness of the business model days before a record listing. That candor is worth something. It also shows how much of this boom rests on predicting demand two years out.

[pauze]

A related voice of concern. Jacob Coxon, a former researcher at OpenAI and Anthropic, says a slide from Anthropic's IPO roadshow was a key reason he left the company after about four months, The Decoder reports. The chart tracks how much compute Anthropic needs over time to train its models. Coxon reads it as evidence that algorithmic efficiency is improving at an accelerating rate, and claims Anthropic could already retrain its Mythos model with a fraction of the compute originally needed. Anthropic notes on the slide that the data points are internal estimates and for illustration only. Coxon wants frontier model development paused next year. That is a minority position, but it shows that the IPO paperwork itself has become ammunition in the safety debate.

[pauze]

Then OpenAI, which has reorganized its robotics team. It was first described as a disbanding, but The Decoder has since updated its story after OpenAI clarified that robotics work continues. Researchers move to the main research team under chief research officer Mark Chen, while the applied robotics and hardware teams join Greg Brockman's applied organization. According to Fortune, as reported by The Decoder, the decision came so suddenly that OpenAI's booth at a major robot learning conference in Austin sat empty.

The reasoning, according to The Decoder, is that general models such as GPT-6 Astra can increasingly control robots directly. In an Nvidia study, an Astra-powered agent reached a success rate of almost 75 percent on simulated robot tasks, against 51 percent for the best specialized models. Critics point out that specialized models may still win on cost, reliability, speed and energy use.

The Decoder also reports on a different approach. Video AI company Runway released its robot model Praxis-1 with open weights under the Apache license, The Decoder reports. It leads the category of so-called world action models on Nvidia's RoboLab benchmark, but Runway ran those tests itself, and they have not yet been independently verified.

[pauze]

In brief.

Google's Gemini 4 Carbon has taken the top spot on the MathArena math benchmark from OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol, with Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 in second, The Decoder reports. With the settings used, Carbon and Fable cost roughly fifteen and forty times as much as GPT-6.1 Sol.

The Financial Times reports that shares in data center construction companies, the picks and shovels of the AI trade, have lost 4.2 trillion dollars in the tech sell-off.

An investigation led by Japan's computer emergency response team has identified about a thousand servers suspected of supporting AI-driven cyberattacks since October, The Business Standard reports, citing The Straits Times. Investigators found traces of a programming tool from Anthropic on one server.

And in the United States, OpenAI has launched a ChatGPT Wallet that lets its AI agents make purchases, Bright reports. It is for Pro subscribers only, and every purchase must be approved with biometric verification.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands, where the news this weekend is about AI in cyberattacks. The Financieele Dagblad reports that a Dutch organization working on cybersecurity, a group of ethical hackers, was itself hit by an AI-driven attack. The organization says it had never seen an attack like it.

That fits a broader European picture. Cloud Expo reports that a large European e-commerce organization has been targeted for days by attacks using ARTEX, an autonomous AI framework in which multiple AI agents work in parallel. The agents explored different attack routes and adapted their approach to the defenses they met, and there are strong indications that other e-commerce companies worldwide were also hit. The affected company has not been named.

For Dutch boards, the lesson is uncomfortable. Attackers now run agents that probe around the clock. Defenses built for human tempo will need to speed up as well.

[pauze]

That's it for this Sunday. Have a good day, and see you tomorrow morning.
