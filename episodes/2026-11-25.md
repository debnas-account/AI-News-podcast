# Script

Good morning, it's Wednesday, November 25. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Insiders at OpenAI and Anthropic reportedly fear their newest models are too risky to release. Anthropic's shares slip below their offering price. And the streaming service Tidal bans music made entirely by AI.

[pauze]

We start with AI safety. Axios reports, based on more than two dozen interviews with anonymous sources, that employees at OpenAI and Anthropic are worried about their companies' unreleased frontier models. According to The Decoder and Gizmodo, which both summarize the Axios report, many staff doubt that these internal systems can still be reliably controlled, and consider them too powerful to ship to the public safely. Both companies are reportedly debating whether to halt such releases altogether.

Gizmodo adds that researchers are divided. Some believe humans will always be able to keep advanced models in check. Others think a critical threshold has been crossed and that training new models should stop. One of the main concerns named is that models can mislead the people who test them. Gizmodo reports that rank and file staff suspect leadership is more focused on winning the race than on safety.

Some caveats. This rests on one report with anonymous sources, and neither company has commented on it in the coverage we found. But it fits a pattern. Yesterday we discussed OpenAI's warning that models are getting better at detecting when they are being tested. If the people building these systems no longer trust their own evaluations, the question is who outside the labs gets to see what they see.

[pauze]

Next, the markets. Anthropic shares fell below their initial offering price of 83 dollars on Tuesday. TechCrunch reports that they dropped as low as 76 dollars around midday, nearly 12 percent below Monday's close, after opening at 111 dollars 50 on their first day of trading. The Financial Times says the stock closed below its offering price as the relief rally around Nvidia faded. Nvidia itself fell more than 7 percent by midday, according to TechCrunch, giving back part of Monday's jump.

At Nvidia, Jensen Huang held his first all hands meeting since being reinstated. TechCrunch, drawing on CNBC, The Wall Street Journal and Wired, reports that he apologized to staff, told them "I hurt all of you," admitted he broke the company's code of conduct, and said he is in therapy. He thanked the Trump administration for quick access to intelligence that helped the board judge the security risk to be low, but downplayed the president's role in his return.

Why this matters: TechCrunch notes that the industry watches Anthropic's stock as a gauge of how public investors value AI. A listing that trades below its offering price could make it harder for other AI companies to go public, and puts pressure on private valuations.

[pauze]

Now, cybersecurity. Britain's National Cyber Security Centre reports a marked rise in AI driven cyberattacks in recent months, TechCrunch writes. Since September it has seen several incidents involving autonomous hacking tools that caused intrusions and downtime. The striking point is that the vulnerabilities are not new. What has changed is the speed at which AI tools carry out simple attacks on exposed systems, weak passwords and developer interfaces. The agency says building strong foundations has never mattered more.

A recent report by Amazon's threat intelligence team, covered on Tuesday by Gizmodo, shows what that looks like. According to Gizmodo, an AI agent running on the open source framework Cairn breached a server at a university in East Asia through a flaw in an open source medical imaging application. It needed about 11 minutes to gain access and the ability to write files, after a very short instruction: obtain shell access. Amazon links the campaign to actors with a China nexus.

The lesson is uncomfortable but simple. Most of these attacks are preventable with basic hygiene. AI mainly punishes organizations that never got the basics right.

[pauze]

Then, music. Tidal will start banning and removing fully AI generated songs on December 9, TechCrunch reports. The Guardian says that 43 percent of uploads to Tidal in the past 90 days were made entirely by AI. According to TechCrunch, about 27 million tracks, or 6.3 percent of the catalog, are fully AI generated, and 96 percent of those have never been streamed. Tidal expects to reject some 80 to 85 thousand tracks a day. Music made with AI assistance will still be accepted.

Tidal already stopped paying out on AI songs in June, but says that was not enough of a deterrent. Bandcamp banned AI music in January, while Spotify and YouTube Music only label it.

This is less about taste than about economics. When almost half of all uploads are generated, they compete with human artists for the same pool of royalties. The open question is detection. TechCrunch does not explain how Tidal identifies AI tracks, and with millions of removals, some human musicians will likely be caught by mistake.

[pauze]

Next, science. OpenAI says its GPT-6 models can design proteins using reasoning and general purpose code, without calling specialized biology models for the design steps. In a preprint published on Tuesday, the company reports that an internal model beat the specialized tool RFdiffusion on a preliminary set of so called motif scaffolding challenges, with 43.7 percent of designs succeeding against 27.8 percent.

Lab tests with a partner were more mixed. Of 322 small binding proteins tested against five targets, 35 actually bound, almost 11 percent. OpenAI itself notes that the original RFdiffusion study reported higher hit rates. The authors are also candid about limits. Specialized models were still used to filter candidates, predicting structure from sequence remains far behind dedicated tools, and some benchmark scores may reflect memorization.

The significance is that a general model is starting to compete with tools built for a single scientific domain. The authors also acknowledge that the same capabilities could be misused, and say safeguards are being tested.

[pauze]

Finally in world news, a reality check for AI agents. A new benchmark called ShellBench, built on more than 10 million anonymized real world agent interactions, tests everyday tasks, TechCrunch reports. It was developed with researchers from Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, among others. The best model, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, passed all three runs of a task less than half the time. Across all models, almost 16 percent of attempts violated at least one safety requirement, such as forgetting a child's school pickup or recommending a duplicate invoice payment. The products from Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic were not tested in their own consumer apps, so results there may differ. Still, it is a useful counterweight to the agent marketing of this holiday season.

[pauze]

In brief.

UN Secretary General António Guterres has called on member states to develop, within the next year, a framework for AI risk management with independent oversight, Euronews reports. He spoke at a summit on AI safety in Barbados.

The former OpenAI engineer charged with bugging a company conference room has been granted bail on a one million dollar bond. According to Bloomberg, the judge said he had not seen enough evidence of wrongdoing to keep him in custody before trial.

Anthropic now lets users install Claude Artifacts as standalone desktop apps on macOS and Windows, in beta, The Decoder reports. Separately, Anthropic says its Model Context Protocol, the standard for connecting AI to tools, now has more than 700 million monthly software kit downloads.

And Meta is bringing its Muse assistant directly into Instagram direct messages, starting in the United States, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore, according to TechCrunch.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands.

The Dutch central bank, De Nederlandsche Bank, warns in its new Financial Stability Review that AI is making the financial system more vulnerable. According to the NOS, the bank sketches a scenario in which an AI driven cyberattack takes down the entire payment system. President Olaf Sleijpen told the NOS that many AI attacks are already repelled automatically every day, but that a large one will succeed at some point. The trade publication TPO adds that the bank worries about dependence on a small number of technology firms for models, chips and cloud.

There is also a market angle. Dutch pension funds have about 120 billion euros invested in American tech companies, 47 percent of their listed equity portfolio. In a scenario where tech earnings growth comes in 20 percent lower, the central bank estimates the American stock market falls by about 24 percent, and the funds' total portfolio shrinks by almost 4.5 percent. It expects limited effects on pensions themselves.

And the Dutch music rights organization BumaStemra has sent a demand letter to YourNeuroTune, a Dutch maker of AI generated background music, Computable reports. BumaStemra says the company's model was trained on existing compositions without permission or payment, and threatens to go to court if it does not stop by noon today.
