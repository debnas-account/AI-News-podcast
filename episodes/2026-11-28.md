# Script

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 28. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. OpenAI hits back at Apple in court. Banned Nvidia chips turn up at a Shanghai data center tied to the woman at the center of the Nvidia scandal. And Germany's cybersecurity agency warns of a flood of autonomous AI attacks.

[pauze]

We start with the legal fight between OpenAI and Apple, which escalated on Friday. OpenAI filed a countersuit against Apple in a California court, accusing it of trade secret theft and unfair business practices. The Financial Times first reported the filing, and The Decoder and Engadget have the details.

According to the complaint, a former OpenAI employee who returned to Apple passed along details about OpenAI's product plans and its suppliers. OpenAI also says Apple coerced and threatened suppliers that work with both companies. Bloomberg describes it as Apple allegedly pressuring suppliers to shun OpenAI's devices. And OpenAI claims Apple encourages staff to keep corporate files on personal devices and cloud accounts, and then uses that against employees who want to leave. Apple had not commented when Engadget published.

Some background. Apple sued OpenAI this summer, accusing it of a coordinated campaign to steal trade secrets through former Apple employees, after OpenAI bought io, the hardware studio of former Apple design chief Jony Ive. The two companies were partners about two years ago, when ChatGPT came to the iPhone.

Why it matters: this is no longer a dispute about a few employees. It is a fight over the supply chain for the next generation of AI devices. If OpenAI's claims about supplier pressure hold up, that raises questions about how much room a newcomer really has to build hardware in a market where Apple is the biggest customer of so many factories.

[pauze]

Next, the Nvidia story that keeps on giving. A Bloomberg investigation, reported by The Decoder and Engadget, found that two Chinese companies installed Nvidia's powerful Blackwell hardware at a Shanghai data center linked to Alice Huang, the woman at the heart of the controversy around Jensen Huang's brief resignation as Nvidia's chief executive.

According to Bloomberg, documents filed with Chinese regulators mention a GB300 NVL72 rack and a GB200 server at the site. One company, Shanghai Shuoyao, offered the GB300 rack as collateral for a loan of about fourteen million dollars. Bloomberg traced the rack through a serial number in a public warranty database to the Taiwanese manufacturer Gigabyte. Shipping these systems to China requires a United States export license.

Shuoyao has ties to Megaspeed, a Singapore cloud company that Alice Huang controlled until August 2025, Engadget writes. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, and Bloomberg found no evidence that it diverted the chips directly. Gigabyte declined to say who originally bought the rack and said it follows applicable laws. Nvidia did not immediately respond to Engadget.

The timing is awkward. Jensen Huang is scheduled to testify publicly before Congress on December 3, according to Engadget. The finding suggests that export controls leak not just through smuggling, but through complex chains of resellers and financiers.

[pauze]

Now to security. Germany's Federal Office for Information Security, known by its German initials as the BSI, warned on Thursday of a sharp rise in attacks using what it calls agentic offensive frameworks. That is according to the Dutch security site Security.NL. These are AI systems that scan targets, find vulnerabilities and carry out attacks almost on their own. The BSI names three: Artex, which we discussed yesterday after Microsoft's analysis, as well as CAIRN and PentAGI.

The agency's point is that an attacker now only needs a target and a short instruction, which makes attacks much cheaper. BSI head Claudia Plattner warned of an avalanche of new cyberattacks and said it is crucial that basic security measures are put in place quickly and consistently. Germany already raised its general threat level on November 19 to tense to critical, partly because of autonomous AI systems.

What stands out is the tone. A national agency is no longer talking about a distant risk but about an immediate one, and as we will hear later, the Dutch are sending a similar message. The worry is that attackers are automating faster than most organizations are patching.

[pauze]

Then a story about what AI coding agents do to business models. Grand Theft Auto 6 launched on consoles with an estimated 3.79 billion dollars in first-day revenue, The Decoder reports. A day later, a hacking group distributed a stolen Xbox build of the game. Less than four days after the executable leaked, a tracking platform counted 40 active projects trying to port it to PC. Nearly half could already boot the game, and gameplay of an unofficial multiplayer mode surfaced on Thursday.

According to The Decoder, AI coding agents such as Claude Code are speeding up this kind of reverse engineering dramatically. Take-Two, the publisher, filed a copyright takedown on GitHub that removed an initial project and more than three thousand copies. Neither Rockstar nor Take-Two has commented publicly.

This matters well beyond gaming. Rockstar's model depends on staggered releases, with the PC version often arriving a year or more later, and on online spending that brings in most of Take-Two's revenue. If coding agents can compress years of porting work into days, any company that relies on technical barriers to control when and where its product appears has a new problem.

[pauze]

Next, education. The Los Angeles Unified School District, with about half a million students, has blocked Google's AI Overviews on school-issued devices, Engadget reports. There was no formal announcement. Parents and students noticed the summaries had disappeared, and the district later confirmed it, calling the change temporary and saying it wants to keep students' experience age-appropriate and safe.

The move followed a district AI committee meeting on November 13. Google's position is that search and AI are deeply integrated, as its head of education told The New York Times. Districts that block the AI features would lose access to Google Search entirely. According to Engadget, New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago are reportedly considering switching search engines if necessary.

This is a test of bundling power. Google is effectively telling schools to take AI with search or leave both. Critics would say that is exactly the kind of tying competition authorities look at. If large districts actually switch, it would be one of the first real costs of that strategy.

[pauze]

In brief.

OpenAI is building a data platform codenamed Athena, The Information reports via The Decoder. It is a semantic layer that gives AI agents standard definitions for business metrics, after at least one customer reportedly found ChatGPT's data agent not accurate enough. That would put OpenAI in more direct competition with Snowflake and Databricks.

Anthropic's shares rose almost 11 percent on Friday to close at 91 dollars and 88 cents, well above the 83 dollar offering price, according to The Motley Fool. The site points to expected buying by index funds, and notes that up to 303 million shares become eligible for sale on December 15.

In Belgium, the Walloon Region has halted construction of a Google data center in Farciennes, Techzine reports. Google kept building after its permit for a modified design was withdrawn in August. Google says the original permit still applies, and the case is now before the Council of State.

And Poland is defending a 300 million dollar deal with Palantir to use its AI software for analyzing battlefield data, according to Politico Europe and Bloomberg. The defense minister said it would be wrong not to use it, despite concerns in Europe about reliance on American technology.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. The National Cyber Security Centre has issued a warning about the risks of growing AI use by companies, Security.NL reports. It responds to the recent figures from Statistics Netherlands showing that AI has moved from experiments to a fixed part of business processes, in marketing, bookkeeping and security, but also indirectly through spam filters and staff using AI tools.

The NCSC lists several risks. Company data can end up with an external AI provider and leak. AI can give wrong advice. AI agents can use personal data they should not have access to, which according to the NCSC can breach the GDPR. And dependence on models and suppliers is growing. Its advice is practical: map which AI tools are in use and which are actually needed, assess the risks, assign clear owners, take measures that match those risks, and review the policy every year.

Together with the German warning, the message is consistent. Adoption is moving faster than governance, and the gap is where the risk sits.
