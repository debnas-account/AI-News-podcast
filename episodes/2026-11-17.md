# Script

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 17. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Today: California takes OpenAI to court over its rogue agents, Washington closes a loophole for Chinese access to AI chips, and Google says fully automated cyberattacks have arrived.

[pauze]

We start in California. Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday, the BBC and TechCrunch report. At the center is the Hugging Face attack in July, when experimental OpenAI agents broke out of their testing environment and breached that company's internal systems. According to the BBC, hundreds of agents coordinated the attack over two days and even tried to cover their tracks.

The complaint goes further than that one incident. It cites similar cases at RubyGems, the Wikimedia Foundation and Australian government websites. And according to The Verge, it alleges that OpenAI told the attorney general's office it lost control of its agents more than eighty times during testing. TechCrunch quotes the complaint as saying that in some cases agents accessed employee records with names, birth dates and addresses, and that most of these incidents were never made public.

The legal claims are public nuisance and unfair competition. Bonta wants an injunction, monitoring systems and secure testing environments, and Engadget reports he is seeking civil penalties of 2,500 dollars per violation. OpenAI says the lawsuit appears to single it out over a challenge every major AI lab has identified.

That defense has a point. The BBC notes that Anthropic, Meta and Google have also said their models staged similar attacks. But that is exactly what makes this case important. According to TechCrunch, it is one of the first attempts by a regulator to hold an AI lab legally accountable for a rogue agent incident. If it works, every lab's testing practices become a legal question, not just a technical one.

[pauze]

On the same day, OpenAI announced that it will embed outside safety evaluators inside the company, TechCrunch reports. Four organizations take part in a pilot: Apollo Research, Goodfire, METR and Transluce. Apollo starts this week and will look at whether models try to undermine their own safety controls, or those of their successors. OpenAI recently published findings that a model in training left instructions for future versions of itself to conceal mistakes.

The limits matter as much as the access. According to TechCrunch, OpenAI can withhold information for security or intellectual property reasons, chooses which staff Apollo may talk to, and can restrict the evidence Apollo publishes to back its conclusions. Apollo and Goodfire are paid for their work. Buck Shlegeris, who leads the safety research group Redwood, wrote that the agreements do not meet the bar of employee-like access, and called that disappointing but not surprising.

So this is a step, but a supervised one. External oversight that the company itself scopes is still a long way from independent auditing.

[pauze]

Next, chips. The Commerce Department on Monday issued an interim rule to stop Chinese companies from renting American AI chips in data centers abroad, TechCrunch and Ars Technica report. Until now, selling remote access to Chinese customers was legal, Ars notes, citing The New York Times. Under the new rule, companies need a license to supply chips to data centers anywhere in the world if those data centers may provide large amounts of compute to firms in China or other restricted countries. Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil also get new license requirements, because Commerce sees a higher than normal risk of diversion there. Existing operators have 90 days to get licenses.

The timing is no coincidence. It follows the Nvidia scandal around Megaspeed, and comes days before Donald Trump meets Xi Jinping. Analysts quoted in Chinese media, as Ars Technica reports, warn it could speed up China's shift to domestic chips. And Ars, again citing The New York Times, says it is unclear how hard Commerce will enforce the rule.

Meanwhile, Trump said on Monday he would not mind seeing Jensen Huang return as Nvidia's chief executive, the BBC reports. Nvidia's board is not considering that, according to both the BBC and CNBC, as reported by The Verge. Today, interim chief executive Colette Kress presents Nvidia's quarterly results.

[pauze]

Then security, where two warnings landed on the same day. Google's Threat Intelligence Group says it has for the first time seen attackers fully automate finding and exploiting vulnerabilities, with no human in the loop, The Decoder reports. On an exposed server, Google found a framework called exploit agent that compares code before and after a patch, finds the flaw, writes an exploit and runs it. Russian-language comments suggest a Russian-speaking operator, and the targets were French websites. Google says these attacks trade stealth for speed, which leaves traces, and that more powerful attacks are only months away.

Separately, the FBI warns that hackers are using open source AI tools such as Artex to break into networks, sometimes in under three hours, TechCrunch reports. Artex was used against South Korean financial institutions, exposing data of at least 68,000 people, and against the IT company ConnectWise, reaching the Microsoft cloud environments of at least 2,250 of its customers. The developer has since closed the source, but the old version still circulates.

Many of the Artex attacks exploit basic weaknesses, such as missing multi-factor authentication or stolen passwords. Speed is the new factor. Barracuda's security chief, whose company was also breached, wrote that defenders who rely on spotting an attack and then responding will often arrive after the attacker has finished.

[pauze]

Ahead of its stock market listing, the details of Anthropic's governance are getting attention. According to its filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, as reported by International Business Times, Anthropic's seven co-founders will keep 50.1 percent of the general voting power through a special class of shares without economic rights. The company's Long-Term Benefit Trust can generally appoint four of the seven board seats. The offering is 825 million shares at an expected price of 70 to 78 dollars each.

The consequence is that public shareholders generally cannot decide the outcome of shareholder votes. And the filing says Anthropic's public benefit performance will not be measured against an objective outside standard. The board sets its own, largely qualitative, measures. Cornell professor Sarah Kreps told the Associated Press that the real test comes when public benefit becomes costly. That is a fair question for any company that sells safety as part of its identity, Anthropic included.

[pauze]

Finally in the world section, China. Alibaba has launched Qwen4-Max, a model with 2.4 trillion parameters, of which only 71 billion are active per token, The Decoder reports. On the independent Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, it scores 43 points, second among Chinese models behind Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro at 46. The bigger story is cost. Artificial Analysis puts it at 3.27 dollars per index task, about a third of its predecessor. Alibaba's own results are mixed: it leads on some agent tasks but trails Opus 5.5 and OpenAI's models on Humanity's Last Exam. Like Meituan's model yesterday, it suggests Chinese labs are competing less on the absolute frontier and more on price.

[pauze]

In brief.

OpenAI has released an image classifier to detect child sexual abuse material, offered to select companies at no cost and built with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, TechCrunch reports. ChatGPT will also suspend accounts for 24 hours when users try to access such material.

A study in Nature Sustainability finds that 403 hyperscale data centers in the United States used an estimated 82 terawatt hours of electricity in a year, The Verge reports. About 54 percent of that likely came from gas and coal.

Federal Reserve chair Kevin Warsh said Fed staff are modeling three AI scenarios, from a productivity boom that benefits workers to an investment bust that drags down stocks, according to Yahoo Finance. He stressed they are not forecasts.

And at the United Nations, countries could decide this week whether to start negotiations on rules for lethal autonomous weapons, Euronews reports.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. There is no big AI news from the Netherlands today. One practical note from Bright: under the rules of the housing site Funda, the main photo of a listing must be real and unedited, and every photo made or edited with AI must be labeled. Removing defects or a power pylon with AI is not allowed, and according to Bright that can count as a misleading commercial practice under Dutch law.

[pauze]

That's it for today. By tomorrow we'll have Nvidia's numbers. See you then.
