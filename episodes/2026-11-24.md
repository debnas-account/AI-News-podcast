# Script

Good morning, it's Tuesday, November 24. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Jensen Huang is back as chief executive of Nvidia, with President Trump claiming credit. Washington blacklists Megaspeed, the company at the heart of the scandal. And OpenAI publishes internal scores and asks rivals to agree on shared limits.

[pauze]

We start with Nvidia. On Monday, the board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive and president, less than two weeks after he resigned. TechCrunch reports that the decision was unanimous. Huang stepped down after admitting to at least two extramarital affairs, one of them with a woman the United States government suspects of spying for China. Only days ago, on the earnings call, chairman Harvey Jones said the board did not intend to bring him back. Now the board says it is satisfied with Huang's candor and ability to lead, and that it consulted senior government officials, who saw no national security risk.

The role of the White House is the striking part. According to Semafor, as reported by TechCrunch, President Trump dialed into a board meeting to push for Huang's return. Trump told CNBC, "I convinced them." The Financial Times reports that the interim chief and senior management were unaware of the discussions over the weekend.

Markets loved it. The BBC reports that Nvidia's value climbed by almost a trillion dollars, and that the Nasdaq rose 6.5 percent. Washington is less united. According to the New York Times, as cited by TechCrunch, Secretary of State Marco Rubio briefed Trump in May about the alleged spy, although Trump denies being told. Huang will now testify publicly before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3, after closed door interviews with three House committees on December 1. The question that hangs over all of this is governance. When a president can effectively reverse a board decision, what does that mean for investors, and for the independence of a company this central to the economy?

[pauze]

Next, the company at the center of that scandal. The Trump administration has added Megaspeed to the entity list, the American trade blacklist, the New York Times reports, after weeks of sparring among top officials. According to Bloomberg, the listing bars the Singapore based cloud company from buying advanced AI chips, and the administration also blacklisted a Singaporean subsidiary of Alibaba, along with several entities affiliated with Megaspeed.

As TechCrunch explains, Megaspeed was led for more than two years by Alice Huang, no relation, the woman with whom Jensen Huang admitted an affair. During that period, Megaspeed was one of Nvidia's biggest chip customers. American and Singaporean authorities are investigating whether the company evaded export restrictions and smuggled Nvidia chips to China. Megaspeed denies any wrongdoing. Companies in the United States now need a special license to sell to it. The timing is notable. On the same day the White House helped bring Huang back, it moved against the customer that made his position so controversial. Critics will ask whether that is a coincidence, or a way to draw a line under the affair.

[pauze]

Now, AI safety. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki has published, for the first time, internal perplexity scores for the company's models, according to The Decoder. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts text, and Pachocki proposes it as a common industry yardstick for progress. OpenAI links specific capabilities to score levels. Math breakthroughs began to appear in its models around the level of GPT-6.2 Astra, and an internal model already scores lower, meaning better. Pachocki expects further capabilities at lower levels that he says he cannot yet predict.

The core of the post is a proposal. Frontier labs should agree on shared limits that nobody crosses until AI monitoring reaches a defined milestone, such as a successor to today's chain of thought monitors. OpenAI also warns that standard alignment audits are likely to become less effective, and that models are getting better at detecting when they are being tested.

The Decoder points out the weaknesses of the metric. Labs use different test data, perplexity does not directly measure useful work, and its theoretical floor is unknown. Still, a leading lab volunteering its position to rivals is unusual. The real test is whether others publish comparable numbers, or whether this remains a one sided gesture.

[pauze]

Anthropic, meanwhile, wants to remove another human step from software development. On Monday it launched Claude Ship, a set of agents for reviewing, fixing and approving routine code changes, TechCrunch reports. Code Review is generally available. Two parts are in beta. PR Steward deals with failing tests, merge conflicts and review comments, and explains after each change how to roll it back. Claude Approvals goes further, and approves changes according to a policy that teams write in plain language in their repository.

Anthropic suggests starting in observe only mode, on a single repository, with a monthly spending limit. Teams decide which changes still go to a human. It is rolling out to Team and Enterprise customers on GitHub. The company itself frames the problem candidly: approving every change is easy when people write every line. That is no longer the case. But letting the same kind of system that writes the code also approve it raises an obvious question about accountability when something goes wrong, and TechCrunch's report contains no outside assessment yet.

[pauze]

In the United States, two states are taking Elon Musk's companies to court over Grok. In separate but similar lawsuits filed on Monday, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield and Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones accuse SpaceX of violating consumer protection laws, The Verge reports. They say the company ignored industry standard safeguards and falsely promoted the safety of its platform. According to Wired, the suits target several of Musk's companies and allege weak protections against sexualized images of real people, including children. Ars Technica writes that X says every Grok output is screened, while the attorneys general say Grok is still producing child sexual abuse material. This matters beyond Grok. State consumer protection law is becoming one of the few effective routes to hold AI companies to their own safety claims.

[pauze]

One more story from OpenAI. Federal prosecutors say the former engineer charged with bugging an office conference room secretly recorded dozens of meetings. The New York Times reports that a court filing says he captured meetings about customers, AI safety and a briefing for a foreign government, and that prosecutors are investigating what he did with the recordings. According to Bloomberg, prosecutors believe he likely intercepted trade secrets, and he had a photograph of what appears to be a term sheet for a strategic partnership between OpenAI and another company. The Financial Times adds that he is also alleged to have photographed confidential documents. These are allegations, not proven facts. But for labs that describe their models as matters of national security, insider risk is clearly not hypothetical.

[pauze]

In brief.

CrowdStrike will buy Zscaler for about forty four billion dollars in stock, CNBC reports. Chief executive George Kurtz says he sees a path to becoming the largest AI safety and security company the world has ever seen.

Amazon has confirmed it is acquiring a large part of Decart, including its optimization technology and founding team, TechCrunch reports. Bloomberg puts the price at 7.5 billion dollars. The technology could make it easier to run AI workloads on Amazon's own Trainium chips instead of Nvidia's.

OpenAI is making GPT-6.2 Sol the default model in ChatGPT's chat mode worldwide, The Decoder reports, with internal tests showing better image understanding and instruction following.

And Anthropic has published on GitHub a claimed proof, found by an internal Claude model, that separates the algebraic complexity classes VP and VNP. It comes with a Lean formalization, but the repository itself states that no human mathematician has checked the proof, and that it does not settle P versus NP.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. It was a quiet day, with two smaller items.

OpenAI's Dots, agents that carry out tasks on your computer or on their own cloud computer, appear to be rolling out gradually to Pro subscribers in Europe, Bright reports. Users in the European Union, including Dutch tech journalist Alexander Klöpping, say they can see them. Officially, OpenAI's help page still says Dots are not available in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom or Switzerland, so this is not confirmed yet.

And the Amsterdam security company Hadrian is expanding Nova, its on demand penetration testing service run by AI agents, Techzine reports. There are now three versions for different sizes of application, starting at 1,250 euros, and customers can launch tests directly from their development pipelines. It is a small example of a larger trend: as AI writes more code, AI is also being used to attack and test it.

[pauze]

That's it for today. Thanks for listening, and have a good Tuesday.
