# Script

Good morning, it's Friday, November 27. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Nvidia pauses server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries, and Chinese AI labs feel the squeeze. Microsoft dissects an AI framework that state hackers use to automate attacks. And the math breakthroughs of the past weeks are turning into an encryption worry. It was Thanksgiving in the United States, so a slightly shorter episode today.

[pauze]

We start with chips. Nvidia has told manufacturing partners, including Dell and Supermicro, to stop shipping servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. The Financial Times first reported it, citing three people familiar with the matter, and TechCrunch, citing the Wall Street Journal, says the pause has no set end date while Nvidia checks whether its regional partners meet United States export requirements.

The background is a tightening by the Commerce Department earlier this month. Chinese companies had been renting Nvidia computing power from data centers in third countries, and that route is now closing. According to The Decoder, cloud providers in the region have ninety days to apply for a license and must show that no restricted companies are among their users. One executive called the situation chaos. Dealers are already offering thousands of B300 chips that were meant for Chinese rental customers, and banks have halted about five hundred million dollars in loans to a Singapore cloud provider that planned to serve ByteDance.

How much does this matter? The think tank Institute for Progress estimates that such remote data centers supply about a quarter of China's AI computing power. And Reuters, as reported by The Decoder, says the Chinese labs Z.AI and Moonshot AI are delaying their next flagship models, citing the cloud curbs, the shipment halt and the crackdown by OpenAI and Anthropic on distillation of their models. Moonshot is reportedly turning to the latest Google and Meta models for training data, and looking for compute in Europe. The open question is whether this slows China down for good, or mainly pushes it faster toward domestic chips.

[pauze]

Next, security. Microsoft's threat intelligence team has published a technical analysis of ARTEX, an AI framework for running cyberattacks with almost no human involvement. The Decoder reports that the code appeared on GitHub in July and was deleted in October, but copies still circulate, and a Chinese firm, ThreatBook, sells a commercial version. ARTEX does not invent new techniques. It uses a set of agents to plan and run existing tools for scanning, password guessing and exploitation, and attackers can plug in any language model, including versions with the safety guardrails removed.

According to Microsoft, state-backed groups from China and North Korea use it, as do criminal gangs. One campaign produced more than one hundred and fifty thousand failed login attempts, at a peak of almost three thousand per minute. In another case, an agent needed five minutes to find exposed credentials and pull data from a cloud key vault.

Microsoft's advice relies on the fact that these tools are still noisy, so threshold filters and decoys can catch them. The Decoder points out the weakness in that: patient attackers who slow down, or models that recognize a decoy, could get around it. It fits a pattern we have covered all week. The cost of attacking keeps falling, while defense still depends on organizations patching faster than ever.

[pauze]

Then a story about mathematics that is really about money and secrets. The Financial Times reports that Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey says unreleased AI models have raised concerns about encryption, as breakthroughs in mathematics stoke fears that data across financial systems is under threat. The full article is behind a paywall, so that is as far as we can go on his exact words.

The same theme appears in a new blog post by the complexity theorist and former OpenAI researcher Scott Aaronson, as summarized by The Decoder. He argues that the claimed AI proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which we covered last week, matters less in itself than the fact that an AI system can produce such work at all. He points to recent OpenAI results on matrix multiplication and the Fourier transform that prove hard limits on how fast certain computations can go. He says these results have changed his view on one of computer science's biggest open problems, and he writes that he is virtually certain the AI labs hold one or more bombshells in cryptography, while adding that he has not seen the details.

That is a strong claim built on inference, not evidence, and it should be treated that way. But when a central banker and a leading theorist voice the same worry in the same week, regulators will want to know what the labs are testing, and who gets told first.

[pauze]

In brief.

SAP is preparing a plan to cut at least ten thousand positions to reshape its software business for the AI era, Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the matter. The company has not confirmed the plan.

The Guardian reports that undisclosed use of AI has affected at least twenty-five court cases in England and Wales this year, with AI-generated witness statements, fictitious case law and statutes that do not exist.

The British financial regulator, the Financial Conduct Authority, has complained that Meta declined a request for joint testing of its personal agent Muse before rolling it out in the United Kingdom on Tuesday, according to Wired.

And in Florida, a man who used an AI clone of a streamer's voice to con a memecoin project out of about two hundred and sixty thousand dollars in cryptocurrency has been sentenced to forty-seven months in prison, Engadget reports.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands.

Statistics Netherlands, the CBS, says forty-one percent of Dutch companies now use AI, up from eighteen percent a year earlier, the NOS reports. Large firms use it most, but small and medium-sized businesses grew fastest. Marketing and sales lead, and use in bookkeeping and financial management rose from seventeen to thirty percent. More than half of the companies using AI report higher productivity, while a quarter see no effect at all.

In Amsterdam, the Dutch consumer association Consumentenbond and two foundations, Offlimits and The Firewall, asked the district court on Thursday to ban Meta's camera glasses. They argue the glasses make covert filming too easy, for example because the recording light is hard to see in sunlight. Meta says the glasses are not built for secret recording, that misuse comes from a small group of users, and that a ban is a decision for politicians, not a judge. The cabinet saw no reason for a ban in September. The ruling is expected by December 24 at the latest.

And in the Dutch House of Representatives, Volt member Dassen has asked the education state secretary to hold Google to account for refusing to let schools switch off the AI mode in Google Search, according to Security.NL. He also asks whether European alternatives should be considered.

[pauze]

That's it for this Friday. Have a good day, and talk to you tomorrow.
