# Script

Good morning, it's Monday, November 16. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Today: Washington turns up the pressure on Nvidia, Jensen Huang reportedly weighs a comeback, a big week for AI on the stock market, and a Chinese open model trained without a single Nvidia chip.

[pauze]

We start in Washington. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Sunday that the real question at Nvidia is not the affair that cost Jensen Huang his job, but whether the company knowingly let its most advanced chips reach China. Speaking on Fox News, Bessent asked whether Nvidia used shell companies to get around American export rules, the news agency AFP reports. He said what matters most is whether Nvidia had a culture of compliance around those restrictions. And he wants answers from Nvidia and its internal investigation within ten days. According to Axios, Bessent also urged the company to hire an outsider as its next chief executive.

Bessent pointed at another government department too. According to the Financial Times, as reported by AFP, four federal agencies wanted the Commerce Department to blacklist the Singapore company Megaspeed, and Commerce did not act. Bessent's words: many other departments were pushing for this, and Commerce had the pen. The New York Times separately reports that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick pushed to weaken chip rules affecting Southeast Asian data centers, some of them clients of his former firm.

Megaspeed is the company once controlled by Alice Huang, the former executive whom American officials suspect of spying for China. On Sunday, Megaspeed said she left the company on August 30, 2025, and has no current role, Euronews reports. Singapore's interior ministry said last week that she was not investigated for espionage, but for suspected offences around buying and exporting servers.

The numbers in question are large. According to documents reviewed by the Wall Street Journal, as reported by The Decoder, Megaspeed arranged nearly twelve billion dollars in Nvidia chip orders for Chinese companies between March 2024 and June 2025. Nvidia says it investigated these allegations last year and refuted them. The Justice Department is running a criminal probe, and Huang and other Nvidia officials have agreed to testify before three House committees, with no date set yet.

What stands out is the shift in framing. This started as a story about one man's private life. It is now a question about whether the most important company of the AI boom took export controls seriously.

[pauze]

Meanwhile, Huang himself may not be done. According to the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the matter, Huang has discussed returning as chief executive with confidants, Yahoo Finance reports. A supporter told the paper that a war room of Silicon Valley executives is being assembled to lobby the board, including seeking support from the White House. The report rests on anonymous sources.

The open letter calling for his reinstatement now has more than 158,000 signatures, according to The Guardian, and AFP puts the number above 165,000. Not everyone is on board. Analyst Dan Ives of Yorkville told Yahoo Finance that talk of a return is ridiculous in his view.

The market stakes are clear. Nvidia's stock fell more than twelve percent last week, according to Yahoo Finance, and at least ten Wall Street analysts downgraded it from buy to hold. On Tuesday after the close, Nvidia reports quarterly results, its first earnings call without Huang.

That makes this a big week for AI on the markets. On Thursday, Anthropic plans its Nasdaq debut, aiming for a valuation of about 1.3 trillion dollars, TRT World reports. We covered the numbers behind that listing yesterday. The new element is the timing: a test of investor appetite for AI, two days after Nvidia opens its books in the middle of a scandal. Not everyone is bearish, though. Morgan Stanley strategists recommend a maximum overweight position in global stocks, Bloomberg reports, citing an intact AI investment cycle.

[pauze]

Next, China, where the chip question looks different. Meituan, best known for food delivery, has released the weights of LongCat-2.5, a model with 1.6 trillion parameters built for AI agents and coding, The Decoder reports. Meituan says it was trained entirely on Chinese hardware. The example setup for running it yourself uses 194 Huawei Ascend accelerators, and the guide contains no instructions for Nvidia graphics chips at all.

On Meituan's own benchmarks, LongCat-2.5 scores 88.4 on Terminal Bench, just behind Claude Opus 5 at 89.1. On another coding test it trails most of the listed models. The weights are under the permissive MIT license, so companies can use and modify the model commercially.

Two caveats. The benchmark figures come from Meituan itself, and one test was run in its own environment. And the comparison is with older Western models, not the newest ones. Still, the signal is hard to miss. While Washington debates how chips leak to China, Chinese companies are showing they can train competitive open models without them.

[pauze]

Then the labor market. Australia's central bank has published an analysis of the early effects of AI on jobs, The Decoder reports. The Reserve Bank of Australia finds that AI has barely displaced jobs so far. But in the occupations most exposed to AI, such as programming, clerical work and customer service, young workers are doing worse. Employment among 15 to 24 year olds in those jobs has fallen since late 2022, while employment among older workers has risen.

The author is careful. The study shows no direct causation, and it measures theoretical exposure, not actual use of AI. Post-pandemic shifts or remote work could also explain the trend. The Decoder places it alongside earlier research from Stanford and Anthropic that found a similar pattern for young people in exposed jobs.

That is the pattern worth watching. The worry is not mass layoffs, but a quietly narrowing entry into the labor market.

[pauze]

Finally in the world section, Apple. The company has launched a study app to learn how people use AI for health questions, TechCrunch reports. Apple works with OpenEvidence, a medical AI company, and the answers draw on journals such as the New England Journal of Medicine, the Journal of the American Medical Association and Nature. The study is open to American users over 21 and aims for up to 200,000 participants over more than a year. Participants rate answers and can choose to share health data from their iPhone.

Apple says data is anonymized and will not be used to train AI models, and that OpenEvidence deletes queries after 24 hours. It is unclear whether this will lead to AI health answers in Siri or the Health app. The approach is notably cautious. Apple is collecting evidence first, while competitors such as OpenAI and Anthropic have already launched health features.

[pauze]

In brief.

The top scorer in South Korea's national exam for tax accountants has admitted to cheating with a hidden camera and AI, the BBC reports. According to The Guardian, he got only one of 160 questions wrong, and there are now fears for the national college entrance exam next week.

Reports of AI being used to sexually exploit children have risen by more than 8,400 percent in two years, according to research from an Edinburgh-based child safety organization, The Guardian reports.

The United States and its allies have agreed to jointly identify and protect AI technologies and supply chains considered vulnerable to China, the Financial Times reports. The pact comes ahead of a meeting between Donald Trump and Xi Jinping at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen.

And OpenAI has launched a science blog. Its first post describes a candidate definition of the Standard Model of particle physics on a lattice, and says its model GPT-6 Astra helped turn the idea into a detailed proof. That is the company's own account.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. There is no major Dutch AI news this morning. The Huang affair is getting analysis here too: NRC argues that the case shows how risky the governance model of the AI sector is, with power at the leading companies concentrated in a small group of people, or often just one.

[pauze]

That's it for this Monday. Have a good start to the week, and see you tomorrow morning.
