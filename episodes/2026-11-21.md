# Script

Good morning, it's Saturday, November 21. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. An Anthropic mathematician claims to have proved the Riemann hypothesis with help from Claude. Wall Street has its worst week since Covid. And President Trump threatens a tax on data centers built abroad.

[pauze]

We start with mathematics. Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, announced on Friday in an essay that he believes he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, working with Claude. The conjecture dates from 1859, concerns the hidden structure behind the distribution of prime numbers, and is one of the Millennium Prize problems. Alpöge writes that the method also resolves the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

According to TechCrunch, the proof comes as code in Lean, a formal proof language that a computer can check mechanically. Several mathematicians, including Andrew Sutherland of MIT, said in an online discussion that they compiled it successfully, and TechCrunch later added that it had been checked by independent sources. The BBC also reports the claim, and notes that verification will take some time.

There is an important caveat. A Lean file that compiles is not yet a proof that people understand. TechCrunch writes that the informal argument is not yet understood well enough to judge, and Alpöge has promised a human readable explanation. He says he messaged Anthropic's chief executive last Sunday about how to announce it, so the timing, right after the company's stock market debut, will draw questions. And in a twist, Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil on Friday posted a separate, entirely human proof of the generalized version by the Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, the result of twelve years of work. Vakil calls it an early draft. Two very different paths, both now awaiting scrutiny.

[pauze]

Next, the markets. Friday was a brutal day on Wall Street. According to NBC News, the Nasdaq Composite fell 5.3 percent and wiped out all its gains for 2026. The S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent and the Dow 1.9 percent, which pushed both into correction territory, ten percent or more below their recent peaks. The Financial Times describes it as the worst week for American tech stocks since the Covid crisis.

Anthropic, on only its second day of trading, fell 9 percent and now sits just a few dollars above its offer price, NBC reports. Nvidia dropped about 14 percent after a Reuters report that regulators are examining possible accounting irregularities at the chipmaker. The New York Times reports that this concerns a probe by the Securities and Exchange Commission.

The causes NBC lists go beyond any single company: doubts about whether the huge AI investments will pay off, slowing data center construction, rising interest rates, and the Democratic midterm victory, which investors fear could slow new data centers. Bloomberg adds that measures of American credit risk hit an eight month high. The open question is whether this is a correction of overheated valuations, or the start of a real cut in AI spending. Those are very different stories for the industry.

[pauze]

That brings us to Washington. At a news conference on Friday, President Trump threatened American tech companies that build AI data centers in other countries. According to the Associated Press, he said: "We're not going to allow companies to build data centers in other countries." He then added that they can, but that he would put "a tremendous tax" on them. He said his own staff were hearing about the plan for the first time.

There were no details on the size of the tax, its legal basis or whether it would apply to existing sites. The New York Times also notes it is unclear how he would carry it out. The context matters. The AP cites its own voter poll showing that about three quarters of voters in this year's election opposed a new data center in their community, and those concerns played a role in the Republican midterm defeat. Trump urged companies to "make the deal" with local communities, through tax breaks or lower utility bills.

For Europe, this is worth watching. If American firms were penalized for building abroad, that would sit uneasily with European ambitions for more AI capacity on this continent, much of which still depends on American investment.

[pauze]

Next, privacy. The messaging app Signal is blocking AI agents built into mobile operating systems from reading its chats. In a blog post on Friday under the name of Meredith Whittaker, Signal explains that on iOS 27 the app now blocks screenshots and offers a setting to block Siri AI. On Android, Signal switches on screen security when it detects an AI assistant trying to read the screen. The company names Google's Gemini, Apple's Siri, Microsoft Recall and Meta's Muse agent.

The reasoning is that these agents capture what is on screen and store it locally or on remote servers, beyond Signal's control, and that affects everyone in a conversation, not just the user who enabled the agent. Signal is open about the limits. The protection does not cover the Mac, it cannot stop agents reading files already on the device, and Android coverage is incomplete. It asks Apple, Google and Microsoft for a simple, documented way for apps to opt out of AI data access. That raises a broader question. As agents get access to the whole device, the separation between apps starts to erode, and app makers have little say over it.

[pauze]

Now, measuring progress. The nonprofit research group METR has updated its widely cited graph of how long the tasks are that AI models can complete. MIT Technology Review reports that some capabilities are still improving along an exponential curve. The update adds tasks that would take people much longer, and more open ended work.

The striking finding is the gap between kinds of tasks. Models now outperform almost every human at software engineering, but they still fall short on work that requires judgment. In one test, models designed AI evaluations, and none was good enough for METR staff to consider useful. On that task, Anthropic's Opus 5.5 reached borderline above the bar, where a year ago every model was clearly below it. METR itself warns that many task durations are estimates, and that there are few judgment heavy tasks in the set. That nuance matters, because this graph is often used as shorthand for how close full automation is.

[pauze]

Then, a fight over copying. OpenAI says it has made it much harder to extract the hidden reasoning of its newest models, GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol. According to The Decoder, the success rate of one attack in OpenAI's own red team tests fell from 54 percent on the previous model to 0.2 percent. The company also says it bans thousands to tens of thousands of accounts every week in coordinated campaigns. The aim is to stop rivals from distilling, that is training their own models on the output of OpenAI's. It shows how much the frontier labs now treat their models' reasoning as a trade secret, which also makes them harder for outsiders to scrutinize.

[pauze]

In brief.

Nvidia is close to buying Inception, a two year old startup that builds language models with diffusion rather than word by word generation, for at least 500 million dollars. That is according to The Information, as reported by The Decoder.

The AI coding startup Cognition is in talks to raise money at a valuation of 75 billion dollars, Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with the matter.

The coding tool Cursor, now part of SpaceX, told American lawmakers it is looking at phasing out Chinese open models, Techzine reports, even though its own Composer 2 model is largely based on Moonshot's Kimi.

And Deutsche Telekom is investing 100 million euros in the French AI developer Mistral, as a first step toward a partnership on sovereign AI for German industry and government.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. Het Financieele Dagblad reports that the Nvidia scandal is being seen in American politics as a wake up call to further tighten export controls on chip machines to China, and that the Netherlands is resisting this with all its might. That suggests the fallout from the Nvidia affair could reach Dutch chip equipment makers, with the pressure now coming from Washington's political reaction to the scandal. Beyond that, there is no other major Dutch AI news today.

[pauze]

That's it for this Saturday. Have a good weekend, and we'll be back tomorrow morning.
