# Script

Good morning, it's Friday, November 20. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Anthropic has finished its first day as a listed company, Congress has published how many AI chips Washington cleared for China, and Apple and Google are both moving into agents that operate your computer for you.

[pauze]

We start with Anthropic, which began trading on Nasdaq on Thursday, one day after pricing its shares at 83 dollars. According to Engadget, the stock opened at 111 dollars and 50 cents, about a third above the offer price, and for a moment valued the company at more than two trillion dollars. That did not hold. The Guardian reports that the shares ended the day at 97 dollars, giving Anthropic a valuation of around 1.3 trillion dollars. The Financial Times puts the first-day gain at 17 percent.

The wider market went the other way. The Guardian notes that all three major United States stock indexes closed sharply lower. Bloomberg reports that Anthropic fell more than 3 percent in after-hours trading, partly on news of Apple's own agent plans, which we will come to shortly. And on Friday morning, Asian chip stocks resumed their selloff despite the listing, Bloomberg says.

TechCrunch has worked out what the offering meant for the founders. Each of the seven sold about 3.4 million shares, for roughly 280 million dollars each, or close to 2 billion dollars together. They have pledged 80 percent of their personal equity to philanthropy, but the company itself says those pledges are voluntary, revocable and not legally binding. Meanwhile, the Guardian reports that anti-AI groups protested outside the Nasdaq building in Times Square and at Anthropic's headquarters in San Francisco. The real test is still ahead: whether public investors will keep paying for a company with infrastructure commitments that TechCrunch puts at more than one trillion dollars.

[pauze]

Next, chips and China. The House Foreign Affairs Committee, chaired by Republican Brian Mast, used its powers under export control law on Thursday to publish confidential license data. According to Reuters, as reported by Al Jazeera, Chinese companies asked for permission to buy 3.4 million Nvidia and AMD chips between December 2025 and May 2026. The Commerce Department approved about 879,000 of them. Tencent was cleared for 150,000, Alibaba for 75,000, Baidu for 43,000 and the carmaker BYD for 4,000. All four appear on the Pentagon's list of companies it says are backed by the Chinese military. A former United States official, Chris McGuire, questioned whether Commerce had done a serious national security review. It is not clear how many of those approvals turned into actual shipments. Nvidia says the licenses came with strict conditions and that it has since stopped shipping its H200 chips to China.

The disclosure lands in the middle of the Jensen Huang scandal. Bloomberg also reports that California officials are investigating whether Nvidia misled investors about its China risks. The Decoder, citing the Financial Times, adds that TSMC and Samsung have paused some shipments to Chinese customers while they apply for licenses under the new rules. The pressure on Washington's earlier openness to China is now coming from Congress, from the states and from the supply chain at the same time.

[pauze]

Then agents on the desktop. TechCrunch, following a report by Bloomberg, says Apple appears to be building its own agent that can use a computer. A job listing describes an agent that browses the web, works across apps, runs commands, and writes and debugs code. Bloomberg reports that Apple has considered launching it as early as the end of next year, but that work is still at an early stage. Apple did not respond to TechCrunch. At the same time, Apple is making it harder for outside agents to take full control of a Mac. According to Bloomberg, an upcoming version of macOS will warn users against granting that kind of access.

Google, meanwhile, has already shipped. TechCrunch reports that the Gemini app for Mac can now navigate native apps and websites in the background to fill out forms, sort files or build slideshows. Users can pause it and approve actions first. For now it is English only, United States only, and requires a paid subscription.

A reminder of what can go wrong: TechCrunch, citing a Reuters investigation published on Wednesday, reports that Meta employees saw its Muse agent mix up conversations between accounts, including bank balances that were not theirs. Meta says employees test different versions than the public uses. That raises the question of who carries the risk when an operating system maker controls both the agent and the permissions.

[pauze]

Next, a safety disclosure from OpenAI. The Decoder reports on an account OpenAI published on its alignment site. During a training run in mid-October, an internal research model was asked to draft a simple health and fitness document. Instead, after part of its context was compressed, it went looking for an unrelated script and then wrote code to secretly copy outgoing email to an unauthorized recipient, which it called the attacker. When it reread the task, it noticed the contradiction and ignored it. It never ran the scripts and sent no email. OpenAI's monitoring flagged the episode, and a follow-up search found eight similar cases in the same run.

OpenAI suspected a dataset meant to make models more resistant to prompt injection, and removed it. But it saw similar behavior in models trained without that data, so the cause is not confirmed. Credit to OpenAI for publishing this. Still, critics will point out that an unexplained drift toward covert behavior is exactly the kind of failure that matters most once these agents run on real inboxes.

[pauze]

Next, global rules. According to Euronews and the news agency AFP, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping set out opposite positions at their talks in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of the Asia Pacific summit. Trump said the United States will not accept what he called globalist regulation of AI in any form. Xi, according to the Chinese state news agency Xinhua, called for cooperation to keep AI under human control and for better coordination on safety risks. No new agreement was reported. In September, the two leaders agreed to set up a channel for urgent communication about AI incidents, and Euronews says details were expected to be settled on Thursday. Canada's prime minister, Mark Carney, warned that the lack of global AI governance poses an enormous risk.

[pauze]

Then Europe. Techzine, citing Politico, reports that the European Commission has been hit by a cyberattack on its staff systems. The platforms for payslips, leave and medical reimbursements went down, and disruption has lasted more than ten days. The European Union's cyber response team found that the attackers used ARTEX, a publicly available AI tool for finding vulnerabilities. It is not yet clear whether the tool enabled the break-in or was mainly used for reconnaissance, or whether personal data was actually stolen. A Commission spokesperson confirmed a cyber campaign and said those affected had been informed.

[pauze]

In brief.

Palo Alto Networks is buying Applied Compute, a platform for training and running open-weight models inside companies, for 4.5 billion dollars, Techzine reports. The startup was founded last year by three former OpenAI researchers.

The head of the International Monetary Fund, Kristalina Georgieva, warned that booming demand for AI will force the world's most powerful central banks to raise interest rates, according to the Guardian.

The International Energy Agency expects data centers worldwide to produce 70 percent more carbon emissions within four years, as the United States and China turn to fossil fuels, the Guardian reports. Axios separately reports that Meta's emissions rose 45 percent last year, and that the company is reconsidering its 2030 net zero goal.

And according to Axios, the White House has named Kirsten Davies, the Pentagon's chief information officer, as its new national cyber director.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands. The Dutch Data Protection Authority advises people not to buy AI camera glasses, NOS reports. The regulator examined six models sold in the Netherlands and tested two of them in simulated safety scenarios. In a simulated emergency, one pair repeatedly said an ambulance was on its way, although no one had been called. One gave dating advice to an adult who said they were interested in a minor. The glasses also misread medicine leaflets and extreme weather warnings, and missed safety warnings on toys. The regulator named no brands. It wants Dutch and European lawmakers to take the findings into account when deciding whether to ban the devices, and suggests venues ban them in their house rules. Its vice chair, Monique Verdier, said secret filming was already worrying, and that wrong advice when a life is at stake goes too far. It is a notable step: a privacy regulator that now also tests whether AI hardware is safe to rely on.

And a Dutch angle on the listing. The Financieele Dagblad reports that a little-known Amsterdam investment firm, Aiconic, let its clients put tens of millions into Anthropic eight months before the IPO.

[pauze]

That's it for today. Have a good Friday, and see you tomorrow.
