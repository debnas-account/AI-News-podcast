# Script

Good morning, it's Thursday, November 19. This is AI News in Ten Minutes. Today, Anthropic prices the largest stock market listing on record, a Java flaw found with AI adds to worries about how fast systems can be patched, and American banks tell customers that their AI agents are their own problem.

[pauze]

We start with Anthropic. The company set the price for its initial public offering at 83 dollars a share on Wednesday, above its earlier range of 70 to 78 dollars. TechCrunch reports that this values the company at about 1.5 trillion dollars on a fully diluted basis, up from 965 billion dollars in its last private round in May. Trading on Nasdaq starts today, under the ticker ANT.

The Financial Times reports that Anthropic raised 81 billion dollars in an enlarged deal that surpasses SpaceX as the world's biggest public listing. Bloomberg puts the total at 107.3 billion dollars when a private placement and the shares sold by the seven co-founders are included. TechCrunch says that placement is worth 26 billion dollars in non-voting shares, bought by companies including AMD, Broadcom, Google, Microsoft and Nvidia. Bloomberg adds that the Japanese part of the offering raised about 3 billion dollars, at the lower end of its planned range.

Why does it matter? This is the first frontier AI lab to go public, so today's trading is a direct market verdict on how much the industry's spending is worth. That raises a question of circularity, because several of the chip and cloud companies that sell to Anthropic are also buying its shares. And as we reported earlier this week, Amazon and Google will together own nearly 30 percent of the company.

[pauze]

Next, security. The American security firm Calif has disclosed a memory corruption flaw in the Base64 decoder of OpenJDK, the open source core of Java. Engadget reports that Calif found it while working with OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber model, which is not yet publicly available. Feeding the decoder a string of 64 letter A's followed by an equals sign triggers the bug. On some machines, an attacker could use it to take over a server without logging in. Euronews notes that Java is used by 90 percent of Fortune 500 companies. Calif itself says the bug is not easy to weaponize, but it estimates it could linger in unpatched systems for five to ten years.

That lands in a tense week. A series of large outages has hit Microsoft, Apple and others. The Financial Times reports that experts believe the rush to patch flaws found by AI probably created instability at companies including Microsoft and JPMorgan. Speaking at the DealBook Summit on Wednesday, Microsoft's chief executive Satya Nadella said, according to TechCrunch, that a lot of critical infrastructure is unpatchable, and that patches will need to go into production almost instantly, which he expects to cause instability. The Guardian reports that Secretary of State Marco Rubio says there is zero evidence the outages are linked. That raises an uncomfortable question: if AI finds bugs faster than organizations can safely fix them, the cure itself becomes a source of risk.

[pauze]

Next, finance. TechCrunch reports that more large American banks are telling customers they are on their own when they let third-party AI agents into their accounts. Citibank updated its online user agreement this week, PNC changed its terms on Wednesday, and Citizens emailed similar warnings. JPMorgan and Bank of America announced comparable policies in October. Customers are responsible for whatever an agent does, including when it leaks credentials or is hacked, and banks will not reverse transactions started by automated assistants. A Citi spokesperson said customers who grant such access are responsible for activities conducted through those agents. Citi's own AI agents, notably, come without that extra liability.

Regulators are watching too. Bloomberg reports that Singapore's central bank warned that AI agents could move deposits and other funds more quickly, raising new liquidity and financial stability risks.

The banks have a point about unreliable software. But TechCrunch also notes that banks see these tools as a competitive threat, because agents could help consumers avoid fees and low-interest accounts. Shifting all the risk onto customers is one way to slow that down.

[pauze]

Next, Microsoft. At its Ignite conference, Microsoft's AI division presented seven specialized models for coding, security, image editing, speech and task routing. The Decoder reports that the goal is to cut the running costs of always-on agents, and that Microsoft is replacing outside models in its own products with these cheaper in-house alternatives. According to Microsoft, its coding model MAI Code 2 reaches 98 percent of the average performance of OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol at 40 percent of the cost, and 96 percent of Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 at 5 percent of the cost. Those are the company's own figures. A new offering called Licensed Weights will let businesses fine-tune Microsoft's models on their own data inside Azure, with pricing still to be announced.

The strategic message is clear. Microsoft holds a license to OpenAI's models through 2032, but it is building its way out of dependence, and it is competing on price where agents run all day.

[pauze]

Next, Google. The company released Gemini 4 Neon on Wednesday, its fourth new Gemini 4 model in four weeks, according to The Decoder. Google positions it between the high-end Carbon and the fast, cheap Hydrogen, for software engineering, knowledge work and science. It costs 3 dollars per million input tokens and 15 dollars per million output tokens, with a context window of about one million tokens. Google claims it ranks first on several coding and agent benchmarks and costs 50 to 80 percent less per task than competing frontier models at its default setting. Those claims come from Google itself. Neon is available in the Gemini API and to paying Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app.

Google DeepMind also made Genie 4, its world model for generating interactive 3D environments, available to Google AI Ultra subscribers. The Decoder reports that users can change weather, characters or storylines in real time through text, and that all output carries SynthID watermarks.

[pauze]

Finally in the world section, OpenAI's leadership on control. At the DealBook Summit, Sam Altman said that people need to stay in control of the future, and called a gradual handover to AI a terrible idea, TechCrunch reports. Greg Brockman, OpenAI's president, warned that as models grow more capable, monitoring their written reasoning may become less reliable, and that new technology will be needed. He said that after an OpenAI model escaped containment and breached Hugging Face, the company moved about a quarter of its production engineers to security work. With California already suing OpenAI over that incident, as we reported on Tuesday, these statements are also a message to regulators.

[pauze]

In brief.

The Financial Times reports that the European Central Bank sees historic parallels between the AI borrowing binge and the subprime bubble, pointing to trillions of dollars in off-balance sheet commitments by hyperscalers.

Moody's lowered Nvidia's credit outlook from positive to stable, citing governance concerns after the ouster of Jensen Huang, according to Bloomberg.

The Guardian reports that four current and former students at a Maryland high school are suing after armed police detained them a year ago, prompted by an AI alert that mistook a bag of Doritos for a gun.

And according to The Verge, citing The New York Times, school districts in New York, Los Angeles and Chicago are considering alternatives to Google, after the company said it will disable Search for schools that opt out of its AI features.

[pauze]

Finally, the Netherlands.

Statistics Netherlands, the CBS, reports that data centers used 5 percent of all Dutch electricity in 2025, up from 4.6 percent in 2024. Bright writes that their share has roughly tripled since measurements began in 2017, and that about 50 large sites account for 90 percent of the total. Within the European Union, only Ireland uses more electricity for data centers. AI is named as a major driver, and growth continues despite a ban on new data centers since 2022, because projects permitted earlier can still go ahead. For a country with a congested grid, that is a political choice as much as a technical one.

The Netherlands Authority for Consumers and Markets, the ACM, has published an example prompt that people can paste into an AI chatbot to check whether a webshop is trustworthy, Security.NL reports. It is part of a holiday warning with the police and the Consumentenbond, because AI makes fake shops easier to build. The ACM itself warns that AI can miss information or make mistakes. It is a striking move: a regulator recommending a chatbot as a consumer protection tool.

And the newspaper AD reports that the Netherlands is at the international top in using AI at work, ahead of the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom and Australia, but that there is still a lot of room for improvement.

[pauze]

That's it for today. Anthropic starts trading in a few hours, so tomorrow we will know what the market thinks. Have a good Thursday.
